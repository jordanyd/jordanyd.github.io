Jordan Weide

Applied Linguistics · LLM Evaluation · Language Assessment

I am an interdisciplinary researcher working across academia and industry. I believe language has the power to shape and facilitate agency and access in people's lives, and AI reflects that same nature. I study how interactional dynamics shape model behavior, and I bring the validity concerns of language assessment to the evaluation of AI.

Research statement

Interaction as a site of evidence

Large language models have moved beyond static repositories of knowledge to become interactive systems capable of simulating purposeful communication. As AI is increasingly understood as a communicative subject rather than only a mediator, applied linguistics, language assessment, and AI research are drawn into closer dialogue, and established theory about human communication can take on new life.

LLM evaluation has begun to recognize the role of interaction, yet practice still centers on output correctness, and limited transparency in how concepts are operationalized risks measuring proxies rather than the concept of interest. Language assessment has long measured interaction, and because its scores carry consequences, its judgments must remain open to scrutiny. LLM evaluations are similarly consequential.

My research brings these measurement practices to LLM evaluation, developing approaches that attend to both interaction and transparency. Real-world adoption of LLMs remains concentrated, and evaluation is an overlooked point of intervention. Transparent, interaction-focused evaluation can build AI literacy, helping people draw on the fuller capabilities of LLMs in their own contexts.

Focus areas

Interaction in evaluation

Assessing how models communicate across turns, alongside the correctness of their outputs.

Transparent measurement

Making explicit how concepts are systematized and operationalized, so judgments remain open to scrutiny.

Consequential validity

Treating evaluations, like test scores, as judgments with consequences for the people they affect.

AI literacy and access

Using transparent evaluation to help users draw on LLM capabilities in their own contexts.

MA Applied Linguistics · University College London · 2022

Development of a Semantic Analysis for Assessment Difficulty Measures

This dissertation investigates whether a test difficulty measure can be built from semantic frame theory. Using the BNC, COCA, and FrameNet through NLTK, I derived the number and mean frequency of frame elements and lexical units for each concept, together with semantic relevance scores, and modeled their relationship to CEFR level with multilevel regression, correlation, and k-means clustering. The study outlines how larger datasets and transformer-based models could extend semantic framing into a transferable analysis of language for meaning-based assessment and timely feedback.
